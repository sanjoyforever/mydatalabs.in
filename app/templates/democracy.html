None of this is a verdict on any country. This index scores what is counted, and the things that matter most — whether courts are independent, whether an election was free, whether a newspaper can print — are not counted by anyone. A high score means the countable things look healthy. Where the counts and the experts disagree, treat both as evidence and neither as the answer.

What this index measures, and what it refuses to

V-Dem, Freedom House and the EIU all build their democracy scores from expert questionnaires: country specialists answer ordinal questions and the answers are aggregated. That method sees things no counter can, and it carries an irreducible coder-subjectivity term that no inter-rater statistic removes.

This index takes the opposite trade. Every input is a count, a rate or a ratio somebody published — votes over voting-age population, seat shares through Gallagher's formula, women's seats over total seats, detainees over prisoners, prisoners over population. Nothing here is an opinion about a country, and no country's classification, regime type or region touches its score.

warning The cost of that discipline

The things hardest to count are often the things that matter most. Judicial independence, press pluralism, media capture, whether an election was actually free — none appear below, because none is a number anyone publishes. An index that only counts what is counted will systematically miss a country that keeps its forms while hollowing out their substance, which is the most common way democracies decline.

Read a high score as the countable things look healthy, not as this is a good democracy. Where this index and a survey-based one disagree, the disagreement is the finding — this one is not the tiebreaker.

Aggregation: why a geometric mean

composite = exp( ∑p wp · ln( max(pillarp, 1) ) )   with  ∑ wp = 1

Indicators average arithmetically within a pillar. The five pillars then combine geometrically, not arithmetically, because an arithmetic mean makes pillars perfectly substitutable — a country can buy back a pillar score of zero with a hundred somewhere else. Democracy does not work that way; the pillars are complements.

This is not hypothetical. Under an arithmetic mean the United Arab Emirates — a federal absolute monarchy with no elected national legislature — scored {% set are = (rankings | selectattr('country_code','equalto','ARE') | list) %} {% if are %}{{ "%.1f"|format(are[0].composite_arithmetic) }}{% endif %}, because a legislative-concentration score of 0 was averaged against an appointed 50% female chamber scoring 100, and the resulting 50 was then averaged away. Geometrically it scores {% if are %}{{ "%.1f"|format(are[0].composite) }}{% endif %}. The switch is the standard fix — it is why the Human Development Index changed in 2010 — and it barely disturbs the league table: the top twenty move by at most one place.

Two indicators were the same indicator

Spearman ρ across the panel, {{ year }}

The source dataset carried twelve scored indicators. Two pairs turned out to be one statistic counted twice:

  • Effective Number of Parties and legislative HHI are algebraically identical — ENP = 10000 / HHI, exactly, for seat shares. They had been keyed independently and disagreed with each other by a median of 6.6% (24% for Brazil), which is a self-inconsistency rather than two readings. Scoring both put party fragmentation into the composite at 15% weight through two different pillars. HHI is now scored; ENP is derived from it and published as context, which also removes the disagreement.
  • Gini and the Palma ratio summarise one Lorenz curve and correlate at ρ = +0.99 here. Gini is scored; Palma is published as context. Scoring both would have double-weighted income inequality inside a pillar that is already 20% of the index.

Which indicators actually discriminate

Provenance: what is measured and what is inferred

{{ "{:,}".format(provenance.anchor_cells or 0) }} of {{ "{:,}".format(provenance.total_cells or 0) }} cells sit on a source

info This panel is not twelve annual series

Twelve indicators for thirty economies over twenty-five years is 9,000 cells. Nobody keyed 9,000 cells. What was keyed is {{ "{:,}".format(provenance.anchor_cells or 0) }} anchor points — a value attached to a year for which a real source exists: an election result, an IPU Parline snapshot, a World Prison Brief report, a World Bank PIP release, a CPJ census — plus a number of series asserted flat across the whole period. Everything between two anchors is straight-line interpolation; everything outside the outermost anchors is held flat.

That is a reasonable way to carry sources that do not publish annually. An election result exists in election years and not between them. It is not the same thing as measurement, and the difference has to survive into the published figures rather than being flattened into a table where every number looks equally solid.

So every row carries its anchor share, the ranking table shows it, and the chart below shows it by year. Where the density is thin, a smooth trajectory is the interpolation drawing a straight line — not evidence of a stable decade.

Known limitations

Each of these is a real defect, not a disclaimer
  • Compulsory voting is scored as civic participation. Belgium, Australia and Brazil post high turnout for a legal reason. No adjustment is made, so the turnout column reads partly as a map of compulsory-voting law.
  • Gender representation cannot tell an elected chamber from an appointed one. The UAE Federal National Council is half women by appointment and scores 100 on that indicator. Geometric aggregation stops it carrying the composite, but the indicator itself is blind here.
  • Incarceration and pre-trial rates reward states that publish low numbers. A state that detains outside the prison system, or reports partially, scores as though it detains little. The pillar reads as carceral burden where statistics are credible and as an artefact where they are not — which is exactly backwards from where it is most needed.
  • More parties is treated as more plural. Legislative concentration scores a two-party system poorly by construction. That is a defensible reading of power dispersion and a contestable reading of democratic health; a stable two-party system is not obviously less democratic than a fragmented multi-party one.
  • Transfer integrity is the one coded judgement here. It is a scored reading of historical events rather than a published count, and 24 of 30 countries sit at 100, so it discriminates only at the bottom of the panel.
  • Zero turnout is recorded as a zero, not as an absence. Years in which a country held no national election carry a 0 that scores as catastrophic participation rather than as a structurally different situation.
  • The sample is the thirty largest economies. That is a selection on GDP, not on population, region or regime, and every panel-wide average inherits it. It is not a world average.
