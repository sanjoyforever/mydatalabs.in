METHODOLOGY

How the Hormuz Crisis Index is calculated

The HMX-INDEX is a weekly composite measuring geopolitical, maritime and energy-market stress in the Strait of Hormuz against a calm baseline. This page documents every input, every constant and every known weakness, so the number can be checked rather than taken on trust.

The formula

Index Score = 100.0 + ∑ [ Weighti × StressScore( Currenti, Baselinei, Capi ) ]

Each component is converted to a stress score between 0 and 100 by expressing its current value as a percentage change from its baseline value, then dividing by that component's cap and rescaling:

StressScore = clamp( ( pctChange / cap ) × 100 , 0 , 100 )   where   pctChange = ( current − baseline ) / baseline × 100

Stress scores are multiplied by their weight and summed, and the total is added to 100. Weights sum to exactly 1.0, so a component at maximum stress across all seven inputs would produce a score of {{ scale_max|int }}.

Directional inversion. For ship traffic a fall below baseline is the crisis signal, so its percentage change is negated before scoring. All other components treat a rise as stress.

The index is one-sided by design. Stress scores are floored at zero, so conditions calmer than the baseline register as 0 rather than as negative stress, and the composite cannot fall below {{ scale_min|int }}. This is deliberate — the index measures crisis stress, not general market wellbeing — but it is a real limitation, and it is listed as one below.

Why each cap was set where it was

Caps are the most consequential judgement calls in the model: a tighter cap makes a component saturate sooner and so raises its effective influence at moderate stress levels. Each is anchored to a historical precedent rather than chosen to make the chart look right.

Baseline selection

For the three market-fed components the baseline is the mean of daily closes across {{ baseline_window.replace('/', ' to ') }} — 20 trading days with no active chokepoint disruption. Ship traffic is baselined on the IMF PortWatch one-year reference window. The remaining three manual components are anchored to their sources' stated pre-crisis levels. No baseline drifts once set.

This is still an approximation. One calm month is a better anchor than one calm week, but a different month would move every historical score by a point or so. A trailing multi-month median would be more robust and is on the roadmap. Until then, the baseline values are published in full — in the table above, in the JSON API, and in the CSV export — so anyone can rebase the series themselves and check how sensitive a conclusion is to that choice.

Data cadence and staleness

Three components — Brent crude, TTF gas and VIX, together 50% of index weight — refresh automatically from market data. The other four have no free API and are entered by hand each week from licensed industry sources. The site does not pretend otherwise: every component in the breakdown table shows its own cadence and last-updated date.

What happens when a value cannot be fetched

  • The last known value is carried forward. It is never reset to baseline — a value we could not fetch is not a value that is calm, and treating it as one would make an outage look like a de-escalation.
  • The component is flagged stale in the breakdown table and in the JSON API.
  • If stale components account for {{ (degraded_threshold * 100)|round|int }}% or more of index weight, the whole snapshot is marked degraded and a reduced-confidence notice appears above the dashboard.

Known limitations

Stated plainly, because a reader who finds these on their own trusts everything else less.

  1. Half the index weight is manually keyed. Four of seven components refresh weekly by hand, not continuously. Between updates those values are carried forward unchanged.
  2. The index cannot express calm below baseline. Stress scores floor at zero, so the composite has a hard floor of {{ scale_min|int }}. A genuine de-escalation below January 2026 conditions is invisible to the model.
  3. The baseline is a single calm month for six of the seven components. See baseline selection above. Ship traffic is the exception: it is baselined on the IMF PortWatch one-year reference window, which is the more robust treatment and the direction the other six should move in.
  4. Components are correlated. Brent (30%), TTF gas (10%) and VIX (10%) tend to move together during an energy shock, so half the index weight can respond to a single event. The composite is therefore less diversified than a seven-component structure implies. A correlation matrix is published alongside the data.
  5. Cap thresholds are judgement calls. They are anchored to historical precedent and documented above, but a different analyst would reasonably choose different values, and the composite is sensitive to them.
  6. Scores are point estimates without a confidence interval. One decimal place implies more precision than four weekly manual inputs can support. Treat differences under roughly one point as noise.
