The Fiscal Dynamics Quadrant

Every year {{ history[0].year }}–{{ history[-1].year }} placed by the two terms that drive the debt ratio: r − g horizontally, primary balance vertically. Up and right are the adverse directions.
Which quadrant this is This plots the two independently measured terms of the debt-dynamics equation against each other — the standard sustainability quadrant in IMF and ECB debt-sustainability work. It is not the quadrant the source material used, which plotted inherited index level against a presidential contribution score; because those two are mechanically correlated (R² ≈ 0.51, see the methodology tab), its quadrants largely sorted administrations by where they happened to start. This one sorts years by the mechanism actually moving the debt ratio.

What Moved the Debt Ratio Each Year

dt − dt−1 = dt−1·(r−g)/(1+g)  +  primary deficit  +  stock-flow adjustment

Loading decomposition chart…

The snowball is what the existing debt stock does on its own at the prevailing r − g; the primary deficit is what the year's policy adds. The two frequently pull in opposite directions, which is why headline deficit figures alone mislead: in FY2022 a {{ "%.1f"|format(decomposition[-4].primary) }}% primary deficit was more than cancelled by a {{ "%.1f"|format(decomposition[-4].snowball) }}pp snowball as inflation eroded the real stock, and the ratio actually fell. The stock-flow adjustment is the residual — borrowing that does not pass through the headline deficit, plus the ~3-month fiscal/calendar offset in these joins. It is plotted rather than absorbed into the other two, because a decomposition whose residual is quietly dropped is one that always appears to close. It averages {{ "%.2f"|format(sfa_mean) }}pp of GDP and is largest in 1946–47 and 2020–21.

Debt Dynamics: Where the Path Goes

dt = dt−1 · (1 + rt) / (1 + gt) + pbt, run forward {{ projection_years }} years under three r − g assumptions
Read the spread, not the line Nearly all of the variation below comes from r − g, a quantity nobody can forecast. Under the favourable path the debt ratio stabilises and the interest burden never reaches the distress zone; under the adverse path it passes it in {{ horizon.thresholds[2].scenario_years.adverse }}. These are fiscal-stress thresholds, not default thresholds. A government that borrows in a currency it issues does not become insolvent at a ratio; what these levels mark is the point at which debt service crowds the discretionary budget out and the remaining options are austerity, inflation, or financial repression. The 35% level is a stated convention here, not an established constant — it is shown because it is the level the original framing used, so the two can be compared.

Reserve-Currency Displacement Simulator

What if the world moves reserves out of the dollar? Move the sliders — the debt-dynamics recursion re-runs live.
What is and is not being modelled The channel here is the one that is actually quantified in the literature: foreign demand for dollar safe assets compresses Treasury yields, and losing it widens them. The default pass-through — {{ "%.1f"|format(sim.passthrough_bp) }}bp of extra borrowing cost per percentage point of reserve share lost — comes from taking ~{{ sim.full_displacement_bp|int }}bp as the cost of losing the privilege entirely (Warnock & Warnock 2009: ~80bp; Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen 2012: ~73bp convenience yield) spread across today's {{ "%.1f"|format(sim.usd_reserve_share) }}pp of allocated reserves. That number is an assumption, not a measurement, so it is a slider too — and the true relationship is very unlikely to be linear, since the marginal effect should grow as the buffer thins.

Not modelled: dollar depreciation feeding import prices and inflation, lost seigniorage, and any disorderly repricing. Each would make the outcome worse, so this is a conservative reading of the scenario.

Why the correction matters Plot each term's raw index change against the level it inherited and you get a strong downward slope: R² = {{ "%.3f"|format(pres.correlations.raw_vs_inherited) }}. Inherit a high number and you are near-guaranteed a "good" score; inherit a low one and you are near-guaranteed a bad one. That slope is regression to the mean, not policy. A 1pp widening of the CBO output gap moves the index {{ "%+.2f"|format(-pres.cyclical_model.beta) }} points. Strongly determined (|t| > 8), so the cyclical share of a term's change is a measured quantity rather than an assumed one.
2. Mean-reversion correction
Δadjusted = {{ "%.2f"|format(pres.reversion_model.alpha) }} {{ "%+.3f"|format(pres.reversion_model.beta) }} × inherited level
n = {{ pres.reversion_model.n }} terms  ·  R² = {{ "%.3f"|format(pres.reversion_model.r2) }}  ·  se(β) = {{ "%.3f"|format(pres.reversion_model.se_beta) }}  ·  t = {{ "%.2f"|format(pres.reversion_model.t) }}
Every point of inherited index level predicts {{ "%.2f"|format(-pres.reversion_model.beta) }} points of subsequent "improvement" for free. Only fourteen observations, so this correction is itself uncertain — which is precisely why the residual carries a ±{{ "%.1f"|format(pres.residual_sd) }} band.
3. What is not estimated
No exogenous-shock term and no inherited-baseline-momentum term. Both are counterfactuals with no estimator on this data. Constructions that include them do so as per-president constants, which makes the resulting ranking an input to the model rather than an output of it. Omitting them is why the pandemic years weigh on the terms that contain them here.

Wars and the Defence Burden

National defence as a share of GDP, {{ history[0].year }}–{{ history[-1].year }}, against the solvency index
Wars enter this index through the debt they leave There is no war component, and no attempt to back a "cost of the war" out of aggregate outlays. Trying the standard method — defence spending above its own three-years-prior baseline — gives sensible answers for Korea and the post-9/11 wars and nonsense for two others: Vietnam scores 0.2pp of GDP because 1962–64 defence was already 10.9% of GDP and the war was absorbed inside a standing Cold War establishment, and the Gulf War scores zero because it was fought during the post-Cold-War drawdown. Both are arithmetically correct and analytically useless, which is the same unidentified-counterfactual problem the presidential panel refuses to pretend it has solved. So this panel reports what is measured — the burden, and the debt across each period — and cites CRS for constant-dollar operation costs rather than deriving a rival estimate.

Which party is better for the public finances?

On this evidence, neither. The mean structural residual is {{ "%+.1f"|format(summary.party_means.D) }} for Democratic terms and {{ "%+.1f"|format(summary.party_means.R) }} for Republican ones. That gap of {{ "%.1f"|format(summary.party_gap) }} points is {{ "%.0f"|format(summary.party_gap_ratio * 100) }}% of the ±{{ "%.1f"|format(summary.residual_sd) }} error bar — far too small to call a difference. What differs is mechanism, not net effect. The record shows Democratic administrations expanding mandatory entitlement commitments without matching revenue, and Republican ones enacting permanent tax reductions without matching spending cuts. Two routes to the same arithmetic. The structural deficit is a bipartisan construction, and the data will not support blaming either side for it.

How did the U.S. get here?

Not through any single decision. The post-war low was FY{{ summary.trough.year }} ({{ "%.1f"|format(summary.trough.score) }}) and the index has risen in steps ever since, with each step tied to a statute rather than an administration: entitlement programmes created without dedicated funding, tax cuts enacted without offsets, and the lapse of the PAYGO rules that briefly forced the two to be reconciled.

The single largest change in the series is recent and mechanical. Net interest now consumes {{ "%.1f"|format(summary.interest_burden) }}% of federal receipts — {% if summary.interest_burden_is_series_high %}the highest in the entire {{ summary.year - 1945 }}-year record{% else %}near the {{ summary.worst_burden_year }} high of {{ "%.1f"|format(summary.worst_burden) }}%{% endif %}, having roughly doubled since 2021 as near-zero-coupon debt from the 2010s rolled over into a higher-rate world.

What does it mean for the rest of the world?

U.S. Treasuries are the global risk-free asset and the dollar is {{ "%.1f"|format(summary.usd_reserve_share) }}% of allocated foreign exchange reserves. The rate the U.S. Treasury pays therefore sets a floor under sovereign and corporate borrowing costs almost everywhere, so a sustained rise in the U.S. term premium is exported rather than contained.

The important detail is that the world currently subsidises this position. r − g is {{ "%+.1f"|format(summary.r_minus_g) }}pp, meaning the debt stock erodes faster than it accrues and the ratio would fall on its own even with a {{ "%.1f"|format(summary.primary_deficit) }}%-of-GDP primary deficit. The last five years make the point starkly: inflation's erosion removed {{ "%.1f"|format(summary.snowball_5y|abs) }} points of debt/GDP while policy added {{ "%.1f"|format(summary.primary_5y) }}. Almost the entire fiscal gap was paid for by inflation rather than by taxation or restraint.

That is a borrowed condition, not a permanent one. The United States currently sits in the “{{ summary.quadrant.name }}” quadrant, which holds only while r stays below g.

How much time is there, and what actually changes it?

Net interest reaches 35% of receipts — the level at which debt service crowds out the discretionary budget — somewhere between {{ summary.fiscal_dominance.earliest }} and {{ summary.fiscal_dominance.latest }}{% if summary.fiscal_dominance.never_count %}, or never under the favourable path{% endif %}. That range, not any single date, is the finding: it is almost entirely a function of r − g, which nobody can forecast.

The two levers are comparable in size, and one of them is not the one usually discussed. Shifting 30 percentage points of global reserves out of the dollar pulls the date forward to {{ summary.sim_full_shift }}. Letting the primary deficit widen to 6% of GDP pulls it to {{ summary.sim_loose_pb }}. Halving it to 1.5% pushes it out to {{ summary.sim_tight_pb }} — a bigger move than de-dollarisation, and the only one entirely within domestic control.

Bottom line

The U.S. fiscal position is strained but not critical, and it is deteriorating slowly rather than approaching a cliff. Its current stability rests on a favourable r − g that is neither guaranteed nor domestically controlled, while the interest burden has already reached a post-war high. The problem is a multi-decade, bipartisan accumulation; the arithmetic that resolves it is the primary deficit, which is the one term in the equation that policy sets directly.

This is an index of structural risk, not a forecast and not investment advice. A government that borrows in a currency it issues does not default at a ratio — what these thresholds mark is the point at which the remaining options narrow to austerity, inflation, or financial repression.

Mathematical Architecture & Methodology

Every figure on this page is derived from published Federal Reserve Economic Data series by scripts/build_solvency_history.py. Nothing is hand-keyed, interpolated or estimated.

1. Composite scoring

USS-INDEXt = 100 + ∑k wk · Sk,t   where   Sk,t = clamp( (Xk,t − bk) / (ck − bk) × 100, 0, 100 )

Each indicator is scored linearly from its baseline bk (0 stress) to a fixed crisis threshold ck (100 stress). Where ck < bk — productivity and per-capita growth — the same expression handles the inversion without a special case.

Why not z-scores and a normal CDF The conventional construction normalises each indicator against its own full-sample mean and standard deviation and pushes it through Φ(·). That has two defects. It is computed in-sample, so every historical value silently restates when a new year arrives, and the index is centred on ~50 by construction — it can say "worse than the post-war average" but never "bad in absolute terms". And a CDF saturates: once an indicator sits two standard deviations out the transform is flat, so the index becomes least sensitive exactly where risk is accelerating. Fixed, argued thresholds keep the scale absolute, keep history immutable, and keep the response linear right up to the cap.

2. Calibration

Baselines are anchored on the measured 1960–2000 United States, not chosen by eye. The two exceptions are marked: the inflation baseline is the FOMC's stated 2% target (a normative anchor, since the 1960–2000 mean of 4.5% includes the Great Inflation), and the primary-deficit baseline is primary balance by definition. As a check on the calibration, FY1965 — the post-war low — scores {{ "%.1f"|format(baseline_check) }}.

4. Debt dynamics

dt = dt−1 · (1 + rt) / (1 + gt) + pbt

d is debt held by the public as a share of GDP, r the effective nominal rate actually paid on the stock (interest outlays over last year's debt, not a market quote), g nominal GDP growth, and pb the primary deficit share. The effective rate converges on each scenario's terminal rate at {{ (repricing_speed * 100)|round|int }}% per year, reflecting the ~6-year average maturity of marketable Treasury debt; the primary deficit converges linearly over ten years. Interest as a share of receipts is then r · dt−1 divided by receipts/GDP, held at the current {{ "%.1f"|format(latest.context.receipts_pct_gdp) }}%.

5. Missing data

The productivity and real-per-capita series begin in 1947, and both are measured as 10-year compound rates, so the growth block is empty for {{ history[0].year }}–1956. Those years are scored on the fiscal and monetary blocks with the weights renormalised to sum to one, and are flagged PARTIAL in the data table. They are not zero-filled: treating a missing growth block as healthy would put an artificial trough at the start of the series, exactly where the post-war debt reduction happened.

6. What this index deliberately does not do

No presidential attribution

A natural extension is to difference the index across presidential terms, subtract adjustments for cyclical and emergency effects, and rank administrations by what remains. That was tested against this series and is not published here, for four reasons.

It mostly measures mean reversion. Regressing such a score on the index level each administration inherited gives R² ≈ 0.51 with a slope of −0.75 — half the variance is explained by nothing but the starting point. Residualising on inherited level reorders the ranking substantially, which means the ranking is not a finding.

The adjustment terms are not identified. The cyclical, exogenous and inherited-baseline corrections are counterfactuals with no estimator. Whether a given emergency package counts as "exogenous" or "structural" is a judgement call that swings the ranking by more than the ranking's own spread — and there is no rule that settles it.

The accounting bases do not match. This index is contemporaneous: it records what a year's data looked like. Claims of the form "this administration created the entitlement problem" require present-value-of-enacted-commitments accounting, under which the 1965 Medicare statute is enormous while the contemporaneous 1965 data barely moves. Those are different models; blending them produces a table whose numbers contradict its own narrative.

n = 14. With fourteen administrations, four-to-eight-year terms and a heavily autocorrelated annual series, differences of a point or two carry no information — and reporting them to one decimal with a "high confidence" label implies precision that does not exist.
